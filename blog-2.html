Sinus headache, migraine or allergy? How to tell

Most 'sinus headaches' turn out to be migraine or allergy. How to tell the three apart, what helps in allergy season, and when endoscopy is useful.

Dr. Arjun VenkateshConsultant · Rhinology & Sleep Surgery
  • 14 Oct 2026
  • 6 min read

"Sinus headache" is one of the most common reasons people come to a rhinology clinic, and also one of the most commonly misdiagnosed. Many people who take repeated antibiotics for sinus headaches actually have migraine, and many others are dealing with allergies that swell the nose every season. Knowing the difference saves time, money and unnecessary medicine.

What true sinusitis feels like

Your sinuses are air spaces in the bones around the nose and eyes. When their drainage openings swell shut, usually after a cold, mucus collects and can become infected. Sinus pain is a pressure in the cheeks, forehead or between the eyes, and it rarely travels alone. Look for:

  • A blocked nose, often on one side more than the other.
  • Thick yellow or green discharge from the front of the nose or down the back of the throat.
  • A reduced sense of smell.
  • Pain that is worse when you bend forward, and a mild fever in the acute phase.

Acute sinusitis usually follows a cold and settles within 10 days to 4 weeks. Symptoms lasting more than 12 weeks are called chronic rhinosinusitis, and that needs an ENT assessment.

What migraine feels like

Migraine can absolutely cause pain around the eyes and cheeks, and it can even cause a runny or stuffy nose during an attack. That is why it is so often mistaken for sinus trouble. Clues that point towards migraine:

  • Throbbing pain, often on one side, that builds over an hour or two.
  • Sensitivity to light, sound or smells, and nausea.
  • Attacks that come and go, last 4 to 72 hours and then clear completely.
  • Triggers such as missed meals, poor sleep, stress, screens or hormonal changes.
  • Clear, watery nasal discharge rather than thick coloured mucus.

A useful test: if your "sinus headache" comes back several times a month, clears completely between episodes and is never accompanied by thick discharge or loss of smell, it is far more likely to be migraine. Antibiotics will not help, but migraine treatment usually does.

Where allergies fit in

Allergic rhinitis is the third piece of the puzzle. In Coimbatore, pollen from grasses and parthenium, house dust mites and the dry winds before the monsoon make allergy season predictable. Allergy causes sneezing in bursts, an itchy nose and eyes, clear watery discharge and a stuffy nose. It does not usually cause a severe headache by itself, but the swelling it produces can block the sinus openings and set the stage for a true sinus infection. That is why many people with allergies get "sinus headaches" every season.

Clues that point to allergy rather than a cold or sinusitis: itching, sneezing fits in the morning, clear discharge, no fever, and symptoms that return at the same time each year or around dust and pets. Children often show it as mouth breathing, snoring and rubbing the nose upwards. Simple measures help most people through the season:

  1. Saline rinses once or twice a day wash pollen and mucus out of the nose. Use sterile, or boiled and cooled, water.
  2. A steroid nasal spray, used daily and aimed slightly away from the middle of the nose, reduces swelling. It takes one to two weeks to reach full effect, so start two weeks before your usual season begins.
  3. Non-drowsy antihistamines help with sneezing and itchy eyes.
  4. Reduce exposure: keep windows closed on windy afternoons, wash pillow covers weekly in hot water, use mite-proof covers and shower after outdoor sport.

Avoid decongestant nasal sprays for more than 3 to 5 days. Longer use causes rebound blockage that can be harder to treat than the original problem. If symptoms persist despite good treatment, skin-prick or blood tests can identify your triggers, and allergen immunotherapy may be an option.

How we work out the cause

At your visit we look inside the nose with a thin endoscope, which takes a few minutes and is done with a numbing spray. This shows swelling, polyps, pus or a deviated septum directly. If the history and endoscopy suggest chronic sinus disease, a CT scan maps the sinuses. If everything looks healthy, we will talk to you about migraine and, where helpful, refer you to a neurologist colleague.

When to see a doctor

  • Nasal symptoms or facial pressure lasting more than 10 days, or getting worse after first improving.
  • Three or more "sinus infections" in a year, or chronic loss of smell.
  • Headaches that have changed pattern, wake you from sleep or are the worst you have had.
  • Allergy symptoms that sprays and tablets no longer control.

Seek urgent care for swelling or redness around the eye, double vision, a stiff neck, confusion or a high fever with facial pain. These are rare but serious complications.
