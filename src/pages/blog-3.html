Teachers use their voice more than almost any other professional: five or six hours a day, often over the noise of a full classroom, a ceiling fan and traffic outside. It is no surprise that teachers make up a large share of the patients in our voice clinic. The good news is that most voice strain is preventable, and most of it recovers with the right habits.

How the voice works, and how it gets tired

Your voice is made by two small folds of tissue in the larynx (voice box) that vibrate hundreds of times a second when you speak. Like any working tissue, they need moisture, rest and good technique. When you speak loudly for long periods, the folds hit each other harder and dry out. Small areas of swelling can form, and with time these can turn into vocal nodules, which are a little like calluses.

Early signs of strain include:

  • A hoarse or husky voice by the end of the school day.
  • Needing more effort to be heard, or a voice that "cracks" on higher notes.
  • A sore, tight or tired feeling in the throat or neck after teaching.
  • Frequent throat clearing, or a feeling of something stuck in the throat.
  • Losing your voice towards the end of the week and recovering on Sunday.

Habits that protect a teaching voice

  1. Hydrate all day. Keep a water bottle on your desk and sip often. The folds are kept moist from the inside, so it takes time; aim for pale urine as a guide.
  2. Do not shout over noise. Wait for quiet, use a hand signal, a clap pattern or a small bell. Move closer to the group instead of raising your voice.
  3. Use amplification. A small voice amplifier with a headset microphone costs less than a course of treatment, and it reduces vocal load dramatically in large classrooms.
  4. Build in voice rest. Plan reading, group work or written tasks between talk-heavy lessons. Avoid long phone calls in your free periods.
  5. Breathe from the belly. Speak on a comfortable out-breath rather than pushing air from the throat, and keep your shoulders and jaw relaxed.
  6. Swallow or sip instead of clearing your throat. Clearing slams the folds together.
  7. Watch reflux. Late dinners, spicy food, tea and coffee late in the day can push stomach acid up to the larynx at night, which irritates the folds.

Whispering is not resting. A forced whisper strains the larynx almost as much as normal speech. If your voice is tired, speak softly in a normal voice, or rest it completely.

What happens at a voice clinic visit

We first listen to your voice and ask about your teaching day. Then we examine the vocal folds with a thin camera through the nose or mouth, often using a strobe light that shows the folds vibrating in slow motion. This lets us see swelling, nodules, polyps or signs of reflux and explain them to you on the screen.

Most teachers do not need surgery. A short course of voice therapy with our speech and language therapist, usually 4 to 6 sessions, teaches efficient voice use and is the main treatment for nodules. Surgery is kept for polyps or cysts that do not settle with therapy, and is done with microscopic instruments as a day-care procedure.

When to see a doctor

  • Hoarseness that lasts more than three weeks, even if it is not painful.
  • A voice that keeps failing by midweek despite rest at weekends.
  • Pain when speaking or swallowing, or a feeling of a lump in the throat.
  • Coughing up blood, difficulty breathing, or a lump in the neck.

Hoarseness lasting more than three weeks should always be examined, especially in smokers. It is usually harmless, but early examination is the best way to catch the rare serious cause.
