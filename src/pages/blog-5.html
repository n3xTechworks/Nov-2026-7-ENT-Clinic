Children today wear headphones for online classes, games, videos and long car journeys. Used sensibly, headphones are fine. Used loudly and for hours, they can cause permanent hearing damage that builds up silently over years. As a paediatric ENT surgeon, I would much rather help parents prevent noise damage than diagnose it afterwards.

How noise damages hearing

Deep inside the ear, the cochlea contains thousands of tiny hair cells that turn sound into nerve signals. Very loud sound, or moderately loud sound for a long time, exhausts and eventually destroys these cells. Unlike skin, they do not grow back. The damage is painless and gradual, so a child will rarely notice it until it is already permanent.

Sound is measured in decibels (dB). Normal conversation is about 60 dB. Many headphones can deliver over 100 dB at full volume, which is as loud as a motorbike close by. At that level, damage can begin in less than 15 minutes.

Warning signs after listening

  • Ringing, buzzing or whooshing in the ears after taking headphones off.
  • Speech sounding muffled for a while afterwards.
  • Your child turning up the television or asking "what?" more often.
  • Difficulty following conversation in a noisy room or classroom.

Temporary symptoms that settle within a day are a warning that the ears were overloaded. Repeated episodes can add up to a permanent loss.

{{icon info 20}}

The 60/60 rule: keep the volume at no more than 60% of the maximum, and take a break after 60 minutes of listening. If you can hear your child's music from an arm's length away, it is too loud.

Practical tips for parents

  1. Choose volume-limited headphones for younger children. Look for a cap of 85 dB, and check that the child cannot easily switch it off.
  2. Turn on volume limits on phones and tablets. Most devices let you set a maximum headphone volume and show weekly listening exposure in their health or screen time settings.
  3. Prefer over-ear headphones to earbuds, and consider noise-cancelling models. When background noise is lower, children choose lower volumes without being asked.
  4. Plan quiet breaks during long gaming sessions or car journeys, and use speakers at home where possible.
  5. Protect ears at loud events. Festivals, firecrackers, weddings with large speaker systems and concerts can all exceed 110 dB. Child-sized ear defenders are inexpensive and effective.
  6. Lead by example. Children copy what they see. Keep your own volume sensible.

For online classes, a laptop speaker in a quiet room is often enough. If your child must use headphones for school, check the volume together at the start of the day and agree on a level that lets them hear the teacher clearly without straining. Teenagers respond better to an explanation than to a rule, so show them the volume warning on their own phone and explain why it appears.

Other reasons a child may not be hearing well

Not every hearing problem in children is caused by noise. The most common cause in young children is glue ear, in which sticky fluid collects behind the eardrum after colds. It often clears by itself within three months, but persistent glue ear can delay speech and affect school work. Wax, ear infections and, rarely, congenital hearing loss are other causes. That is why a proper hearing test, rather than guessing, is so important.

At Tridya, our audiology team tests children of every age, from newborn screening to play audiometry for toddlers and standard tests for school-age children. The tests are quick, painless and designed to feel like a game.

When to see a doctor

  • Your child complains of ringing in the ears or muffled hearing that lasts more than a day.
  • Teachers report that your child is inattentive or misses instructions.
  • Speech seems delayed or unclear for their age.
  • Ear pain, discharge, or repeated ear infections.
  • Any sudden hearing loss in one or both ears, which needs to be seen urgently.
