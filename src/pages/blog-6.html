Hearing loss usually arrives so slowly that the people around you notice it before you do. On average, adults wait seven to ten years between first noticing a problem and having it checked. A hearing test takes less than an hour, involves no needles or discomfort, and gives you a clear picture of what is happening and what will help.

Signs it is time for a hearing test

  • People seem to mumble, especially women's and children's voices.
  • You struggle to follow conversation in restaurants, family functions or meetings.
  • Family members comment that the television is too loud.
  • You find phone calls tiring or avoid them.
  • You hear ringing or buzzing (tinnitus) in quiet rooms.
  • You feel exhausted after social events from the effort of listening.

What happens during the test

Your appointment starts with a short conversation about your hearing, health, noise exposure and the situations you find hardest. We then look into your ears to make sure wax is not blocking the canal. The tests themselves take place in a sound-treated booth.

  1. Pure-tone audiometry: you wear headphones and press a button each time you hear a beep, from very low to very high pitches. This shows the quietest sounds you can hear at each frequency.
  2. Bone conduction: a small vibrator behind the ear sends sound directly to the inner ear. Comparing the two results tells us whether the problem is in the outer and middle ear, or in the inner ear and nerve.
  3. Speech testing: you repeat words at different volumes, which shows how clearly you understand speech, not just whether you hear it.
  4. Tympanometry: a soft probe gently changes the air pressure in the canal to check how well the eardrum moves and whether there is fluid behind it.

We go through your audiogram with you at the same visit, explain it in plain language, and give you a printed copy. It is also uploaded to your patient portal.

Before your test: avoid loud noise such as concerts or power tools for 16 hours beforehand, bring a list of medicines, and if possible bring a family member. A familiar voice helps us with some tests, and they will often remember details you do not.

Do I need hearing aids?

Not everyone with a hearing loss needs hearing aids. If the cause is wax, an infection or fluid behind the eardrum, our ENT doctors can usually treat it directly. For permanent inner-ear hearing loss, which is the most common type in adults, hearing aids are the most effective treatment.

Modern hearing aids are small, discreet and far more capable than many people expect:

  • They are programmed to your own audiogram, boosting only the pitches you need.
  • Many connect by Bluetooth to your phone and television.
  • Rechargeable models remove the need to change small batteries.
  • Directional microphones and noise programs help in busy places.

Treating hearing loss is not only about hearing. Research links untreated hearing loss with social isolation, low mood and a higher risk of cognitive decline in later life, which makes early testing worthwhile.

Getting used to hearing aids

Your brain needs a few weeks to adjust. Everyday sounds such as footsteps, the fan or paper rustling can seem loud at first. We recommend wearing your aids for a few hours a day and building up to all waking hours over two to three weeks. Follow-up visits for fine-tuning are included, and most people find the second and third adjustments make the biggest difference.

When to see a doctor

  • Hearing loss or tinnitus in only one ear.
  • Sudden hearing loss over hours or a few days. This is an emergency; call us the same day.
  • Ear pain, discharge, or dizziness with hearing changes.
  • A child who does not respond to sounds or whose speech is delayed.
