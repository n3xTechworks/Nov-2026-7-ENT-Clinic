Snoring is often treated as a joke or a nuisance for whoever shares the room. Sometimes that is all it is. But loud, nightly snoring can also be the sound of obstructive sleep apnoea, a condition in which the airway repeatedly closes during sleep. It is common, it is under-diagnosed in India, and it is very treatable once it is found.

Why we snore

When you fall asleep, the muscles of the throat relax. In some people the soft palate, uvula, tongue base or tonsils then narrow the airway enough that breathing makes the tissues vibrate. That vibration is snoring. Things that make it more likely include:

  • Weight gain, especially around the neck.
  • A blocked nose from allergies, a deviated septum or swollen turbinates.
  • Large tonsils, a long soft palate or a small lower jaw.
  • Alcohol or sedatives in the evening, and sleeping on your back.
  • Age, as muscle tone in the throat reduces over time.

When snoring becomes sleep apnoea

In obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), the airway does not just narrow, it closes completely for ten seconds or more. Oxygen levels drop, the brain briefly wakes you to reopen the airway, and the cycle repeats, sometimes dozens of times an hour. Most people have no memory of waking. What they notice is the effect the next day.

Signs that suggest OSA rather than simple snoring:

  • Your partner notices pauses in breathing followed by gasps or choking.
  • You wake unrefreshed even after seven or eight hours in bed.
  • Daytime sleepiness, dozing in meetings or, worryingly, while driving.
  • Morning headaches, a dry mouth, or getting up to pass urine at night.
  • Poor concentration, irritability or low mood.
  • High blood pressure that is hard to control.

Why it matters: untreated moderate or severe sleep apnoea is linked with high blood pressure, heart rhythm problems, stroke, type 2 diabetes and road accidents. Treating it often improves blood pressure and energy within weeks.

How we diagnose it

The first visit includes a full ENT examination: nose, throat, tonsils and jaw, plus a nasal endoscopy to look for blockage. We also ask you to complete a short sleepiness questionnaire. If OSA is likely, the next step is a sleep study. For most adults this is a home sleep test: you take a small kit home, attach a finger sensor, a nasal tube and a chest belt, and sleep in your own bed. The data shows how often you stop breathing and how low your oxygen falls.

In selected patients we also perform drug-induced sleep endoscopy (DISE), a short procedure under light sedation that shows exactly where the airway collapses. This guides surgery when surgery is the right choice.

Treatment options

  1. Lifestyle changes: losing even 10% of body weight, avoiding alcohol for three hours before bed and sleeping on your side can reduce snoring noticeably.
  2. Treating the nose: sprays for allergies, or a septoplasty or turbinate reduction if structure is the problem, make breathing easier and help other treatments work.
  3. CPAP: a quiet machine that gently blows air through a mask to keep the airway open. It is the most effective treatment for moderate and severe OSA.
  4. Mandibular advancement devices: a custom mouthguard that holds the lower jaw forward, useful for snoring and mild OSA.
  5. Surgery: for carefully selected patients, such as those with large tonsils, procedures on the palate, tonsils or tongue base can open the airway.

When to see a doctor

  • Your partner has noticed breathing pauses or choking during your sleep.
  • You feel sleepy during the day, especially while driving.
  • Snoring started or got louder alongside weight gain, high blood pressure or diabetes.
  • A child snores most nights, breathes through the mouth or is restless in sleep. Children with OSA often have large tonsils or adenoids, and it can affect growth and behaviour.
