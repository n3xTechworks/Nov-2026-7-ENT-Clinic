What cotton buds really do inside the ear canal, and the safe way to deal with a blocked ear. ---

Almost every week someone sits in our clinic with a blocked ear, a sore canal or a sudden drop in hearing, and the story usually starts the same way: "I was only cleaning it with a bud." Cotton buds feel hygienic, but for most people they are the main reason ears stop cleaning themselves properly.

Earwax is not dirt

Earwax (cerumen) is made by glands in the outer third of the ear canal. It traps dust, keeps the skin of the canal moist and has a mild antibacterial effect. The skin of the canal also grows outwards, slowly, like a conveyor belt. Jaw movement from talking and chewing helps that belt carry old wax to the opening, where it dries and falls out without you noticing.

In other words, a healthy ear is self-cleaning. The only part that needs washing is the outer ear you can see, and a damp cloth on your finger does that well.

What a cotton bud actually does

A bud is wider than the narrow part of the canal. Each time you use one, a little wax comes out on the tip, but much more is pushed deeper, towards the eardrum, where the canal has no glands and no conveyor belt to move it back out. Over months, this builds into a firm plug.

  • Impacted wax: a dull, blocked feeling, muffled hearing and sometimes ringing (tinnitus) or mild dizziness.
  • Canal infections: the bud scratches the thin skin, and moisture plus a scratch is an easy route for bacteria and fungus. This is "otitis externa", which can be very painful.
  • Eardrum injuries: a slip while someone bumps your arm can push the bud through the eardrum. Most small tears heal, but some need repair.
  • The itch cycle: rubbing removes the protective wax layer, the skin dries and itches, and the itch makes you reach for another bud.
{{icon info 20}}

The simple rule we give patients: do not put anything smaller than your elbow in your ear. That includes cotton buds, hairpins, keys, matchsticks and "ear candles", which have no proven benefit and can cause burns.

Safe ways to look after your ears

  1. Leave the canal alone. Wipe the outer ear after a bath with a soft towel or cloth.
  2. If wax builds up, two or three drops of plain olive oil or a pharmacy wax-softening drop, once a day for 5 to 7 days, often lets it work its way out naturally. Lie with the ear facing up for a few minutes after the drops.
  3. Dry ears gently after swimming by tilting your head, or use a hair dryer on the lowest, coolest setting held at arm's length.
  4. Do not use drops if you have a known hole in the eardrum, grommets, or ear discharge, unless your ENT doctor advises them.
  5. Hearing aid users are more prone to wax build-up, because the mould blocks the natural exit. A check every 6 to 12 months is sensible.

How we remove wax at the clinic

When wax does need removing, we look into the ear with a microscope and use gentle suction (microsuction) or fine instruments. It takes a few minutes, you can see what we are doing on the screen, and there is no water involved, which makes it safer for people with a history of eardrum problems. Most patients notice their hearing clear immediately.

Syringing with a large metal syringe is no longer recommended. If you have been told to "get the ears washed" somewhere without a proper view of the canal, it is worth having a specialist look first.

When to see a doctor

Book an ENT appointment if you notice any of the following:

  • Ear pain, swelling of the canal, or discharge that is yellow, green, bloody or smells bad.
  • Hearing that has dropped or felt blocked for more than a week, especially after using a bud.
  • Ringing, a spinning sensation, or a feeling of fullness that is new.
  • Bleeding from the ear after an injury or after cleaning it.

A sudden loss of hearing in one ear, with no cold and no obvious cause, is an emergency. Treatment with steroids works best within the first 72 hours, so call our 24×7 line rather than waiting for wax drops to work.
